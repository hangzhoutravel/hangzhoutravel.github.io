How to Meet People While Traveling Solo in Hangzhou

  • Home
  • Travel Blog
  • Blog Details

Hangzhou is one of those rare cities that feels both ancient and alive at the same time. The mist rolling off West Lake at dawn, the scent of Longjing tea leaves drying in the sun, the neon glow of night markets along Hefang Street—it’s a place that invites you to slow down, breathe, and connect. But if you’re traveling solo, that connection can feel elusive. You might find yourself wandering the Broken Bridge alone, sipping tea by yourself at a湖畔 teahouse, or snapping photos of the Leifeng Pagoda without anyone to share the moment with. The good news? Hangzhou is actually one of the most social cities in China for solo travelers. The locals are curious, the expat community is tight-knit, and the city itself is designed for serendipitous encounters. Here’s how to turn your solo trip into a people-filled adventure.

Start at the Source: West Lake

West Lake isn’t just a scenic spot—it’s a social hub disguised as a UNESCO World Heritage site. The lake attracts everyone from tai chi practitioners at dawn to young couples renting paddleboats at sunset. The key is to position yourself where interaction is natural.

Join a Morning Tai Chi or Qigong Group

Every morning, especially between 6:00 AM and 8:00 AM, you’ll find clusters of locals practicing tai chi, sword dancing, or doing fan exercises near the lake’s edge, particularly around the area near Baidi Causeway and Su Causeway. Don’t just watch—join in. Most groups are incredibly welcoming to foreigners. Simply smile, bow slightly, and mimic their movements. Within five minutes, someone will likely approach you, offer a correction, or ask where you’re from. Even if you don’t speak Mandarin, the shared physical activity breaks the ice. One tip: bring a small gift like a pack of local snacks or postcards from your home country. It’s a gesture that goes a long way in Chinese social culture.

Rent a Bike and Find a Cycling Buddy

Bike rentals are everywhere around West Lake, and cycling the 10-kilometer loop is a favorite local pastime. Instead of renting a private bike, look for the public共享单车 (shared bikes) like HelloBike or Meituan. Why? Because you’ll see other solo travelers doing the same thing. Strike up a conversation at a bike rack: “Hey, do you know the best route to avoid the crowds?” or “Is this bike app working for you?” It’s a low-pressure opener. I once met a graphic designer from Shanghai this way, and we ended up cycling together for three hours, stopping for street food and photo ops. The shared bikes also force you to use your phone, which is a natural conversation starter if you’re struggling with the app.

Take a Sunset Boat Ride

The traditional wooden boats on West Lake are romantic, but they’re also surprisingly social. Many boat operators offer shared rides for about 40-60 RMB per person if you’re willing to join a small group. You’ll be seated with 3-4 other passengers, and the confined space makes conversation inevitable. The boatman often tells stories about the lake’s legends, which gives you a natural topic to discuss with your fellow passengers. If you’re lucky, you might even get a group that wants to split a bottle of local beer or tea afterward. The best time is just before sunset, when the light turns golden and the mood is universally relaxed.

Dive into Tea Culture

Hangzhou is synonymous with Longjing tea, and tea culture here is not just about drinking—it’s about ritual, connection, and slowing down. This makes it one of the easiest ways to meet people.

Visit Longjing Village (龙井村)

Longjing Village is about a 30-minute bus ride from the city center, and it’s where the famous Dragon Well tea is grown. The village is touristy, but in a good way—you’ll find family-run tea houses where the owners are eager to share their knowledge. Walk into any tea house, and the owner will likely invite you to sit down for a free tasting. This is your moment. Ask questions: “How is this year’s harvest?” “What’s the difference between pre-Qingming and post-Qingming tea?” The owners love explaining their craft, and if you show genuine interest, they might invite you to join them for a meal or introduce you to other travelers who are also tasting. I once spent an entire afternoon with a retired couple from Beijing who were on a tea pilgrimage. We exchanged WeChat IDs and still send each other photos of our tea setups.

Take a Tea Ceremony Class

Several places in Hangzhou offer short tea ceremony workshops, often in English. Dragon Well Manor and the China National Tea Museum are solid options, but smaller studios like Tea Drunk or Hangzhou Tea Arts are more intimate. In a class of 6-8 people, you’re forced to interact. You’ll learn how to brew tea properly, but more importantly, you’ll learn how to talk about tea. The instructor will ask you to describe the aroma, the taste, the aftertaste—and that opens up conversations about your personal preferences, your home country’s tea culture, and your travel experiences. By the end of the class, you’ll likely have exchanged contact info with at least two people.

Hang Out at a Teahouse with a Book

This might sound counterintuitive, but teahouses in Hangzhou are surprisingly social spaces for solo travelers. Places like Qing Teng Tea House or the湖畔居 (Lakeside Teahouse) have communal tables where locals and travelers sit together. Bring a book or a journal, but keep your body language open—don’t wear headphones. Order a pot of tea (usually around 80-120 RMB for a pot that you can refill with hot water all day), and settle in. Locals will often strike up conversations if they see you reading something interesting. I’ve had discussions about Chinese literature, travel photography, and even cryptocurrency just by having a visible book. If you’re shy, simply smile and nod when someone makes eye contact. In Chinese teahouse culture, a shared pot of tea is an invitation to talk.

Explore the Night Markets and Street Food Scenes

Hangzhou’s night markets are chaotic, loud, and absolutely perfect for meeting people. The energy is infectious, and the shared experience of eating strange, delicious food creates instant bonds.

Hefang Street (河坊街)

Hefang Street is the most famous pedestrian street in Hangzhou, and it’s packed with food stalls, souvenir shops, and street performers. The trick is to go during peak hours (7:00 PM to 9:00 PM) when it’s crowded enough that you’ll naturally bump into people. Start at the entrance near Wushan Square and work your way through. Stop at a stall that’s drawing a crowd—like the one selling stinky tofu or fried scorpions on a stick. The shared reaction of disgust and delight is a great conversation starter. Say something like, “I can’t believe I’m about to eat this,” and see who laughs. I once met a group of Korean tourists this way, and we ended up spending the entire evening hopping from stall to stall, using hand gestures and Google Translate to communicate.

The Night Market Near Zhejiang University (Zijingang Campus)

This is a hidden gem. The area around Zhejiang University’s Zijingang campus has a student-focused night market that’s less touristy and more authentic. The food is cheaper, the crowd is younger, and the atmosphere is incredibly friendly. Students here are often eager to practice their English, so don’t be surprised if someone approaches you and asks, “Where are you from?” or “Do you like Chinese food?” The best spots are the skewer stalls (chuan’r) and the bubble tea shops. Grab a seat at a communal table, and you’ll likely end up sharing a plate of lamb skewers with a group of students. They’ll probably insist on paying for your food—it’s a Chinese hospitality thing. Accept graciously, and offer to buy the next round.

Late-Night Hotpot (火锅)

Hotpot is a social activity by design. You sit around a bubbling pot of broth, cooking meat, vegetables, and noodles together. It’s impossible to eat hotpot alone without feeling a little sad, but it’s also impossible to eat it with strangers without becoming friends. Look for hotpot restaurants that offer individual pots (like 呷哺呷哺 or 海底捞) but have communal seating areas. Sit near a group of locals or other travelers, and ask for help with the menu. Chinese menus are often overwhelming, with dozens of options and no pictures. Say, “I’m new here. What do you recommend?” This is a guaranteed way to start a conversation. Within minutes, someone will be showing you how to mix your dipping sauce, explaining the different broths, and pouring you a glass of beer. By the end of the meal, you’ll have WeChat contacts and possibly an invitation to karaoke.

Use Technology to Your Advantage

In China, social connections often happen through apps and platforms that are different from what you’re used to. Embrace them.

WeChat (微信)

WeChat is not just a messaging app—it’s the backbone of social life in China. Before you even arrive, join WeChat groups related to Hangzhou. Search for “Hangzhou expats,” “Hangzhou travel,” or “Hangzhou language exchange” on platforms like WeChat itself or on Facebook groups. Once you’re in, introduce yourself: “Hi everyone, I’m [name] from [country], traveling solo in Hangzhou for [duration]. Any recommendations for meeting people?” You’ll get responses within hours. There are also WeChat groups specifically for hiking, photography, board games, and even pub crawls. The Hangzhou Hiking Group, for example, organizes weekend trips to nearby mountains like Mogan Mountain or Tianmu Mountain. Join one, and you’ll instantly have a group of people to explore with.

Meetup and Couchsurfing

Meetup.com has a surprisingly active Hangzhou chapter. Look for events like “Hangzhou Language Exchange,” “Hangzhou International Dinner,” or “Hangzhou Weekend Hiking.” These events are usually held in cafes or co-working spaces, and they’re designed for mingling. The language exchange events are particularly good because they pair you with locals who want to practice English. You’ll spend 30 minutes speaking English, then 30 minutes speaking Chinese. It’s structured, low-pressure, and you’ll leave with at least three new contacts.

Couchsurfing is another option, even if you’re not looking for a place to stay. The Hangzhou Couchsurfing community organizes weekly meetups at bars or parks. Check the “Events” tab on the Couchsurfing app. The meetups are usually casual—people just hanging out, drinking beer, and chatting. I’ve met travelers from all over the world at these events, and many of them became friends I later visited in their home countries.

Douban (豆瓣) and Xiaohongshu (小红书)

Douban is like China’s version of Reddit, and it has groups for everything. Search for “杭州同城活动” (Hangzhou local events) or “杭州徒步” (Hangzhou hiking). You’ll find posts about art exhibitions, book clubs, and even board game nights. The language barrier might be tricky, but the posts are often short and visual. Use Google Translate or ask a local to help you understand.

Xiaohongshu (Little Red Book) is a lifestyle platform popular among young Chinese women, but it’s also a goldmine for travel tips. Search for “杭州独自旅行” (solo travel Hangzhou) or “杭州交友” (make friends in Hangzhou). You’ll find posts from other solo travelers sharing their experiences and sometimes organizing meetups. The comment sections are active, so you can reply to a post and ask if anyone wants to grab coffee.

Stay in Social Accommodations

Where you stay in Hangzhou can make or break your social experience. Choose wisely.

Hostels with Common Areas

Hangzhou has several hostels that are known for their social vibe. The Hangzhou International Youth Hostel near West Lake is a classic choice. It has a rooftop terrace, a bar, and a common room where travelers gather every evening. The staff organizes activities like dumpling-making nights, calligraphy workshops, and pub crawls. Book a dorm bed instead of a private room—you’ll be forced to interact with your roommates. Even if you’re an introvert, the shared space makes it easy to join a conversation. Just sit in the common room with a beer, and someone will eventually say, “Hey, where are you from?”

Boutique Hostels with Events

Newer hostels like the Heyuan Hostel or the Watermill Hostel focus on curated experiences. They offer free walking tours, tea ceremonies, and cooking classes. These events are designed to bring guests together. Sign up for everything, even if you’re tired. The walking tours, in particular, are great because you’ll spend 2-3 hours with a small group, exploring the city and chatting along the way. By the end of the tour, you’ll have a group of people to grab dinner with.

Guesthouses Run by Families

If hostels aren’t your style, consider a family-run guesthouse (民宿) in a village like Longjing or Manjuelong. These are often homes converted into guesthouses, and the family lives on the premises. You’ll eat breakfast with them, and they’ll likely invite you to join their dinner or watch TV with them in the evening. The language barrier can be challenging, but the warmth is universal. I stayed at a guesthouse in Manjuelong where the grandmother insisted on teaching me how to fold dumplings. We didn’t share a common language, but we laughed and pointed and somehow communicated. It was one of the most memorable experiences of my trip.

Embrace the Unexpected

Some of the best connections happen when you least expect them. Hangzhou is full of small, serendipitous moments if you’re open to them.

Get Lost in the Alleys of the Old City

The area around the Drum Tower (鼓楼) and the Southern Song Imperial Street is a maze of narrow alleys, hidden temples, and tiny shops. Put away your phone and wander aimlessly. You’ll stumble upon a mahjong game being played by elderly locals, a calligraphy class in a courtyard, or a group of children flying kites. Stop and watch. If you linger long enough, someone will invite you to join. I once watched a group of older men playing Chinese chess in a park near the Drum Tower. After 10 minutes of silent observation, one of them gestured for me to sit down and offered me a cigarette. We couldn’t speak each other’s language, but we played a few games using only gestures and smiles. It was pure connection.

Attend a Free Concert or Festival

Hangzhou has a vibrant arts scene, and many events are free. Check the website of the Hangzhou Grand Theatre or the Zhejiang Art Museum for listings. During the West Lake Music Festival (usually in October), there are free outdoor concerts at the lake. Bring a blanket, sit on the grass, and strike up a conversation with the people next to you. Music is a universal language, and the relaxed atmosphere makes it easy to talk.

Volunteer for a Day

If you have extra time, consider volunteering. Organizations like the Hangzhou Animal Rescue or the Hangzhou Environmental Protection Center welcome short-term volunteers. You’ll spend the day working alongside locals, which naturally leads to friendships. Even a few hours of volunteering can give you a sense of community and purpose.

Final Tips for the Solo Traveler

Meeting people in Hangzhou requires a mix of intentionality and spontaneity. Here are a few practical tips to keep in mind:

  • Learn basic Mandarin phrases. “你好” (nǐ hǎo), “谢谢” (xiè xiè), “多少钱” (duō shǎo qián), and “你从哪里来” (nǐ cóng nǎ lǐ lái) will get you far. Even mispronouncing them can be a conversation starter.
  • Carry business cards or a small notebook. If you meet someone and exchange WeChat, write down their name and where you met. You’ll thank yourself later when you’re trying to remember who is who.
  • Be open to invitations. If a local invites you to join their table for dinner or to visit their family’s home for tea, say yes. Trust your instincts, but know that Chinese hospitality is genuine and generous.
  • Embrace the awkwardness. Not every interaction will be smooth. You’ll have conversations that fizzle out, moments of silence, and misunderstandings. That’s okay. The beauty of solo travel is that every encounter is a story, even the awkward ones.

Hangzhou is a city that rewards those who linger. It’s not about ticking off sights—it’s about sitting still long enough to let the city reveal itself. And when you do, you’ll find that the people here are just as beautiful as the lake. So put down your phone, step out of your comfort zone, and let Hangzhou introduce you to the world.
